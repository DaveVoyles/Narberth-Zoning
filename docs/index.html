    Charts and graphics

    How residents responded, by zone

    Start with the bar chart — that's your headline number. Then use the other charts to understand the gap between zones, the specific issues people raised, and how confident the analysis is before you cite any figure officially.

    Who opposed, who supported, who was neutral?

    Start here. This is the bottom line — what share of residents in each zone opposed, supported, or were unclear about the proposed changes. If you only look at one chart, make it this one.

    Are the two zones reacting the same way?

    This shows where Zone 4A and Zone 5B diverge. A large gap means the two areas may need to be addressed differently — the same response won't land the same way in both neighborhoods.

    Every response, as a dot

    Each dot is one real response. The size of each cluster makes the scale concrete — not just a percentage, but how many individual people are behind it. A large cluster of red dots means a lot of residents said the same thing.

    The numbers in plain text

    Prefer plain numbers over bar charts? Here's the same data written out. Useful for copying into a report or presenting without graphics.

    How reliable are these classifications?

    Some responses were harder to classify than others. Before citing specific figures in an official document, it's worth having a person double-check the medium- and low-confidence rows — they may need a closer read.

    Topic insights

    What specific concerns did people raise?

    These tags flag the issues mentioned most — parking, height, density, traffic, and more. One person can mention several topics, so these counts show how often a topic came up, not how many unique people raised it.

    Topic cloud

    What dominated the conversation?

    The bigger the word, the more often it came up. This is the fastest way to see what residents are most likely to bring up at a public meeting — the biggest words here are the ones you should be prepared to address.

    Topic by stance

    What drove opposition vs. support?

    Opponents and supporters often worried about different things. Darker cells mean more responses. Use this to understand why people opposed or supported the changes — not just that they did.

    Not sure which zone covers which neighborhood? This maps each zone to the survey pages and street areas it covers. Use it to connect a chart number back to a real place. Note: this is about survey sections, not parcel boundaries.

    Detailed findings

    Current high-level readout

    For both zoning sections, the largest category is Against proposed Zoning Changes. Zone 4A shows a stronger against share than in-favor share, with relatively few neutral responses. Zone 5B also shows more against than in-favor responses, though its in-favor share is higher than Zone 4A.

    Commonly mentioned topics include parking requirements, building height, density, traffic, nearby residential streets, SEPTA assumptions, development scale, green space or impervious surface, and preserving Narberth's small-town character.

    This readout summarizes expressed respondent sentiment so Borough Council, residents, and planners can compare resident feedback with separate planning, affordability, density, or county-level goals.

    Methodology and limitations

    How to interpret this site

    Classification method

    Responses were parsed from the requested PDF page ranges and classified by stance toward the proposal as written. Mixed responses were classified as against when they rejected core proposal elements such as height, density, parking reductions, apartments, ADUs, setbacks, lot coverage, mixed-use changes, or affordability bonuses.

    Limitations

    These responses are written public-comment style feedback. They may not represent all Narberth residents, are not necessarily votes, may include repeated respondents, and rely on judgment for mixed or ambiguous comments.
